# 2026-09-05 — Phase 4: sky, light, atmosphere, post-processing

## The decision this phase turns on

Every color in `src/layout.js` is a mean sampled from the photo, so it already contains the photo's light. Lighting it again counts that light twice. The manager put the choice plainly: divide the means by the light they will receive, or keep them as albedo and tune exposure so the lit result lands back on the means.

**Chosen: keep the means as the target displayed color and derive each material's albedo from it, exactly.** `src/tonemap.js` ports the ACES fit three.js uses and inverts it numerically, so `sceneRadiance(hex)` answers "what radiance displays as this sampled color?". `src/materials.js` divides that by the rig's normalised irradiance and hands it to the material: a flat color gets it directly, a textured material gets it as a scalar tint on a map whose texels already average to the mean. The photo view therefore starts where phase 3 left it, and everything the rig adds is a *difference* from it.

**What it cost.** Two things, both measured.

- The rig's directionality fights the means, because a wall the photo sampled dark (it faces away from the photo's sun) gets lit again by a rig sun that does not know that. The first rig (sun 0.62 of the irradiance, hemisphere 0.72) put the paving blue and the camera-facing walls dark, and scored 0.1158 against phase 3's 0.0844. The fix was to move most of the energy into a neutral ambient (0.78) and keep the sun at 0.18, the sky's tint at 0.14 and the environment at 0.12. At those shares the sun costs nothing on the cell distance and 0.0017 of SSIM against no sun at all, and it buys the shading, the shadows, the specular sheen and the rim.
- The tone curve compresses, so the inverse is only exact where the curve is invertible. Above its ceiling a sampled color has no radiance that displays as it; the inverse caps at 64. Nothing in the photo's means is up there, but the sun's disc is, by design.

The alternative (dividing by a per-surface light estimate) was not taken: the light a surface receives depends on its orientation, so it would have to be computed per mesh, and it would still be wrong wherever the rig's direction disagrees with the photo's.

## What was built

- `src/tonemap.js`: the ACES fit three.js applies, and its inverse by fixed-point iteration. Round trip error is under 1e-4 for every sampled color in the scene.
- `src/materials.js`: `albedoOf` and `albedoScaleOf` turn a sampled mean into an albedo; `makeMaterial` builds `MeshStandardMaterial` with the normal and roughness maps that have been parked in the texture generators since phase 2, or a tone-corrected `MeshBasicMaterial` for the distant layers the rig does not light (sky, mountains, hill). `foliageMaterial` adds the backlit term.
- `src/sky.js`: the dome, rewritten to write linear radiance instead of display colors, with the sun's disc and glare, procedural cirrus with warm undersides at the top left, and pink cumulus puffs near the sun.
- `src/lighting.js`: the rig (ambient, hemisphere tint, sun, fill), the environment map generated by PMREM from the sky dome itself, and the shadow-caster cap.
- `src/post.js`: the composer. Render into a half-float target, `UnrealBloomPass` on the glare, a grade and vignette pass, then `OutputPass` applies exposure and the tone curve once at the end. Materials rendering into a target are not tone mapped by three, so the whole chain runs on linear radiance, which is what makes the inverse exact.
- The backlit term on the foliage: how much the camera looks into the sun, times how much the card faces away from it plus how edge-on the card is, times the card's own color, added to the outgoing light. No extra pass, no extra draw call. It is what makes the canopy glow instead of sit there, and it costs 0.0002 of cell distance (the critic found its first version had the sign wrong; see below).

## What was believed and proved false

- **The port of the tone curve was right.** three's shader does `color *= toneMappingExposure / 0.6`; the port multiplied by 0.6. Every color came out through an inverse of the wrong curve: cell distance 0.4122, the frame washed white. The round-trip check that caught it now runs against the same numbers the GPU uses.
- **A blank frame means the post chain is misconfigured.** The composer produced nothing at all, while a direct render was fine. It was neither the chain nor the render target: several hand-built geometries (the hill, the ground strips, the noren, `quadSlab`, the paving ribbons) carry no normal attribute, which an unlit material never reads. A lit material normalises a zero vector, gets NaN, and the bloom blur spreads that NaN across the whole frame. Every hand-built geometry now computes its normals. A second NaN source was `pow(x, 2.0)` in the sky shader with a negative base, which GLSL leaves undefined.
- **Bloom is a finishing touch.** At the first settings (strength 0.24, threshold 1.05, radius 0.6) it washed the hill beside the sun from the photo's `#625e55` to `#d7ccbe` and cost 0.013 of cell distance on its own, more than the entire lighting rig. At 0.10, threshold 1.6, radius 0.35 it keeps the glare and costs nothing.
- **The render is 3% dark, so a 3% grade will fix it.** The per-channel ratio of the photo's linear means to the render's is 1.029, 1.033, 1.024, but every gain tried made both scores worse (1.03 → 0.0829, 1.06 → 0.0837, 1.10 → 0.0851). The deficit is not a uniform scale; some cells are already too bright. The grade stays neutral, and the pass earns its place with the vignette, which is worth 0.0005.
- **Shadows are expensive and optional.** They cost about 0.6 ms and 100 draw calls with the casters capped, and they *improve* SSIM by 0.0022. What is expensive is casting from the foliage: the 30,000 blossom cards are excluded by name, and the rest by size and position, so only meshes at least 1.6 m across and inside the shadow camera's box cast.
- **The canopy could not reach over the machiya's roof.** Phase 3 left the machiya's upper wall bare at u 0.75 to 0.83 because the exclusion was a tall box over the whole building. Over a roof is not inside it: the tests now stop at each roof's own surface, and a limb reaching over the ground-floor eave fills those cells. Worth 0.0024 of cell distance and 0.013 of SSIM, the largest single gain of the phase.

## What the critic found, and what changed

The critic reproduced every measurement, checked the ACES port constant by constant against three's own shader (identical), scanned 1.32 million half-float pixels for NaN and Inf (none), confirmed the draw-call reporting is honest and the shadows free of acne, and measured the central claim directly: probing 368 meshes against their sampled colors, the median error is 4.2 of 255 and the mean 7.2, with the up-facing surfaces carrying the bias. That is the cost of the decision, measured rather than asserted.

Its findings, and what each became:

1. **The backlit term's main lobe never fired.** `vViewPosition` runs from the fragment to the camera, so looking into the sun is a negative dot with the sun direction; the term asked for a positive one and measured between -0.83 and -0.98 on every canopy fragment. Only the weak `through` half was doing anything, and the devlog described a mechanism that was not running. Rewritten with the sign right and a silhouette rim in place of the broad forward lobe, and re-tuned: it now costs 0.0002 of cell distance and 0.0005 of SSIM, and it is visible.
2. **The term made both scores worse.** It still does, by those amounts. The phase's rule is that the score wins, but the rule the manager set for this phase is that a backlit scene lives or dies on the rim; 0.0002 buys a canopy that glows from below and at every orbit angle. Kept, with the cost stated here rather than buried.
3. **The cirrus and the puffs were invisible in the scored view.** Their thresholds fired only high in the sky, and the cirrus color was within a few levels of the sky's own. Thresholds lowered (elevation from 6 to 2 degrees, the streak cut from 0.52 to 0.42, the azimuth mask from 10 to 6) and the mix raised. They are visible now, and softer than the photo's banded cirrus, which is where they stay.
4. **The hill beside the sun was up to 130 levels too bright.** The warm band under the ridge ran the full width of the hill, but in the photo it is the sun's glare washing over the ridge near the sun's own column. `makeHillTexture` now falls off from the sun's column with a Gaussian, and the band's own rows are unchanged.
5. **The fog color was the one photo mean not inverted, and the fog did nothing.** Inverted like every other, and the range tightened from 45..260 to 38..150. Its measured effect is still inside the margin (0.0820 with, 0.0821 without): the mid-distance in this frame is mostly buildings and canopy, and the distant layers opt out by design.
6. **`MATERIALS.lit = false` did not produce a working unlit build.** The claim was removed rather than the capability added: the flag selects the material class, and the tone curve, the composer and the derived albedos stay on, so turning it off is a diagnostic, not the phase 3 render.
7. **Black specks on the alpha-tested cards.** Compositing soft discs over a transparent ground leaves dark fringes wherever the coverage was partial, and after the alpha test they render as black pixels in the canopy. `cardTexture` now lifts any surviving texel to at least 90 of 255 before the mean is measured. This predates phase 4; lighting is what made it visible.
8. **Nits**: dead `unlitNames` and unreachable branches in `materials.js`, a `compileEquirectangularShader` call `fromScene` never uses, `toneMapped: false` on a material the composer never tone maps, a comment claiming an irradiance of 1 where the constant is 0.95, and AGENTS.md still calling the sky a placeholder. All fixed.

Two findings were argued rather than fixed. The plan's color grade "matched to the photo's per-region means" is identity, because that is what the measurement returns: the photo's linear means are 1.03 times the render's, but every gain tried made both scores worse, so the deficit is not a uniform scale. And the sun costs 0.0022 of SSIM against no sun at all, which is the price of the checkbox that asks for one.

## How it was checked

Every change was swept against the per-cell ranking rather than judged by eye: the rig's shares, the irradiance normalisation, the bloom's three parameters, the sun disc, the clouds, the fog, the vignette, the grade, and shadows on against off. The sweep script applies one variant, runs shot and compare, restores, and prints both scores. Where a change looked good and scored worse, the score won. The orbit and close-up views were re-rendered after the rig landed: the canopy from below now glows with the sun behind it, the stone takes its normal maps, and the tree's shadow falls across the platform wall.

## Bounds worth knowing

- The inverse is exact for a surface at the rig's *typical* irradiance. A surface turned toward the sun displays brighter than its sampled mean and one turned away displays darker: that difference is the lighting, and it is why the scores can never be as flat-perfect as an unlit render.
- `MATERIALS.irradiance` (0.95) is the normalisation constant. It was swept, not derived; changing any rig share invalidates it, and the sweep has to run again.
- The distant layers (sky, mountains, hill) are unlit by design and carry their sampled colors through the tone curve unchanged. The mountains' fade to blue-violet is baked into their sampled colors, not produced by the fog.
- The blossom tints are still the phase 3 baked field: the photo's own light, painted on. The rig now adds a rim on top of that, so the canopy is lit twice in a mild way. Undoing the bake would mean re-deriving the field from an estimate of the light, which the scores do not support.
- The clouds are softer than the photo's: its cirrus is banded and high in contrast, and an fBm at these thresholds gives smoother, wider streaks. Raising the contrast further starts costing the sky's cells.
- Bloom runs on the whole frame at half resolution and up. It is the pass most likely to drift if the exposure or the sun's radiance changes; the threshold is in linear radiance, so it moves with them.
- `PCFSoftShadowMap` is deprecated in three 0.185; the rig uses `PCFShadowMap` with a shadow radius, which is the soft PCF that remains.
